On the ordinary business of weather

Rain seems simple at a distance. A gray cloud gathers, droplets fall, the ground grows dark, and the person at the window decides whether to take a coat. But anyone who has maintained a garden, watched a stream after a storm, or tried to keep books dry in an old house knows that rain is less an event than a sequence of transactions between air, water, stone, soil, and living things.

The first transaction is with the air. Moisture gathers because temperature and pressure change what the air can hold. Clouds are not sacks with loose seams. They are regions in which innumerable small droplets or ice crystals coexist with moving air. When droplets grow large enough, their descent becomes more persistent than the movements that held them aloft. What reaches the ground is still subject to wind. A window may remain dry on one side of the house while rain finds every crack on the other.

A second transaction takes place with soil. Dry earth is not merely empty earth waiting to be filled. Its pores contain air, and its ability to absorb water depends upon their size, their continuity, and the condition of the surface. A hard crust may refuse the first rain, allowing water to run off before it has soaked in. A bed of loose organic material may hold moisture near roots longer than bare sand, although even such material can become waterlogged. The gardener who waters by the calendar rather than by the condition of the soil is sometimes watering what is already wet.

There is an important difference between the appearance of water and its availability to roots. The top of a pot may look dry while the lower portion remains saturated. The opposite can occur when water passes quickly through a channel between the compost and the wall of the container. The useful question is not whether the surface looks tidy but whether the plant can draw moisture from where its roots are actually growing. The answer can change with heat, season, pot size, and the plant's rate of use.

The same patient attention is useful in the house. A wooden window frame swells with dampness and contracts as it dries. This can make a latch difficult one week and loose the next. A cloth left on a chair may draw moisture from the air without looking visibly wet. Books are especially sensitive to repeated dampness because paper and adhesives respond differently to changing humidity. The sensible keeper of a small library makes room for air to circulate, checks for condensation, and avoids pressing an old binding against a cold exterior wall.

It is tempting to imagine that there is a perfect arrangement of domestic things that would eliminate all uncertainty. There is no such arrangement. The most one can do is learn the particular house. A draft from one window may dry a room and cool it too much. Closing the window may make the room comfortable but trap steam after cooking. The solution is not a single permanent instruction but an occasional judgment informed by observation.

Rain also has its intervals. A shower may pass in five minutes, leaving only the smell of wet pavement and a line of drops beneath the eaves. A slow afternoon rain can alter the acoustic character of a house for hours. The steady sound of water on a roof suppresses distant noises while making small nearby sounds feel unusually distinct. A clock can seem louder when the storm stops, not because the clock has changed but because the background has fallen away.

The smell people associate with the start of rain is not produced by a single substance. The earth releases volatile compounds as it becomes damp; the air may carry traces of plant oils and other materials that have accumulated during dry weather. None of these observations is remarkable in isolation. Taken together they explain why an open window can make the same familiar room seem abruptly different after a summer shower.

Animals respond to these changes according to their own needs. Worms may appear on wet ground. Birds alter their foraging and sheltering habits. Insects emerge after rainfall when conditions favor them. A pond changes temperature more gradually than the air above it, and fish may respond to light and water quality in ways that a person watching the surface cannot immediately infer. One ought to be cautious about interpreting every small movement as a signal of happiness, hunger, or distress. It is more useful to establish patterns and recognize departures from them.

There is a quiet pleasure in keeping accurate observations. Record the day, the approximate temperature, whether rain fell briefly or steadily, and what the soil felt like after an hour. Return the following morning and compare what you expected with what you find. A few weeks of such notes can make a garden legible without making it predictable. This is not a scientific instrument of great sophistication, but it is a beginning that distinguishes memory from assumption.

The best advice I can offer a reader who has arrived here in search of a rigid watering schedule is to abandon that hope. Look at the plant, touch the earth, consider the season, and then decide. If you are wrong, observe the result and change the practice. A garden is not improved by being managed relentlessly. It is improved by attention that has learned when to intervene and when to leave things alone.
