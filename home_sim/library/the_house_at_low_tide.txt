THE HOUSE AT LOW TIDE
An original short story written for the Cozy Corner test library

I.

The house had never been handsome, though from the road it enjoyed the advantages of distance. Its roof had the color of an old coin, its garden gate stuck when the air was wet, and its windows looked over a patch of marsh where the sea came and went with the patience of something that had nothing to prove. There was one narrow room upstairs which the former owner had called the observatory. In fact it contained a chair, a small table, and a window with a broken latch. The new owner, Mara, liked the discrepancy. It made her feel that names could be invitations rather than statements of fact.

She arrived in the last week of October carrying a suitcase, a parcel of books, and a box labeled KITCHEN that contained no kitchen things at all. At the bottom were twenty-three letters she had not answered. The letters had followed her from one apartment to another, each new address an opportunity to mistake postponement for a plan. She put the box under the stairs and carried a kettle into the kitchen. The kettle was the only possession she knew exactly where to put.

In the morning the tide was out. Beyond the fence, the marsh lay flat and shining, divided by little channels. A heron stood in one of them with its head pulled close to its shoulders. It seemed to be waiting for a conclusion which might take hours. Mara watched for ten minutes before recognizing that she too had been waiting, not for the bird to move but for the morning to begin in a way she could recognize. The bird remained still. The morning went on without assistance.

There was no heating upstairs. The previous owner had left a stove in the sitting room, a basket of dry logs, and a handwritten warning that the chimney smoked when the wind was from the east. Mara read the note twice. It was not a mystery. It was an ordinary defect, made bearable by the fact that someone had written it down.

II.

She began to take inventory. Three cups, one with a chipped handle. Four plates, mismatched. A little cupboard with hinges that squealed and a shelf in the sitting room that leaned at an angle because the left bracket had come loose. She found a tin of buttons, a folded map, and a pencil reduced to the length of a fingernail. In the pantry there were two unopened jars of marmalade. On the underside of the lid of the first someone had pasted a paper label: APRIL. The second said OCTOBER, though the handwriting was different.

At noon the local shopkeeper came up the path. His name was Elliot, and he had the composed expression of a man who had seen too many people arrive at the coast with a belief that a view would change their habits. He brought bread and a bag of apples, which he put on the table beside the kettle. He noticed the letters in a heap by the stairs but did not mention them. Mara was grateful for the omission, though it left the letters exactly as they had been.

"The gate needs lifting before you turn the latch," Elliot said on his way out. He showed her with one hand, raising the wood until the hinge fell into place. It was such a small correction that Mara felt foolish for how much it pleased her. She had considered buying a new latch. Now she could work the old one. The gate clicked shut behind him with a sound she could hear from the kitchen.

The light changed after three. It crossed the wall, climbed the back of the chair, and then disappeared into a band of shadow beneath the bookshelf. The house smelled of damp wood and apples. Mara unpacked three books. The fourth had been packed last, a thin volume of poems with a green cloth cover that had once belonged to her mother. She held it for a while, then placed it on the table without opening it.

That evening the chimney smoked. The wind had shifted east. For a moment she was angry with the dead stranger who had left a warning but not fixed the chimney. Then she opened the back door and let the smoke escape. In the cold draft she saw the difference between an annoyance and a catastrophe. The kettle still worked. The bread was still fresh. The smoke eventually thinned.

III.

On the third morning, the heron was gone. Mara noticed its absence before she noticed the sunlight. She had expected the bird to be there, though nothing in the bird's life had offered her such a promise. The empty channel made her oddly uneasy. She made tea and watched a crab move sideways through the mud, pushing up a delicate ridge with its claws.

She took the poetry book to the upstairs room and read until the words lost their edges. At first she was tempted to count pages. Counting would make the morning useful, and she had become practiced at making time prove itself. But she had not come to the coast to set an attendance record. She put the book down with the ribbon between two pages and stayed in the chair. Across the marsh the water crept back into the channels, quietly reversing the shapes she had studied.

No one came to the door. No letter arrived. The whole afternoon seemed to exist without asking permission. Later, when she tried to remember what she had done, she could list the tea, the book, the gull at the window, and a brief sleep. Yet the afternoon had a weight she could not account for by adding its events together. She knew she had been present for it. That was not the same as having accomplished anything.

The next day she wrote a letter, not one of the twenty-three, but to Elliot at the shop. She thanked him for the gate. She was embarrassed by the letter even as she folded it. A small thing had happened and she had let it become a small piece of her life. This was unfamiliar, and perhaps the unfamiliarity was the point.

Before bed she went downstairs and opened the box under the stairs. The old letters were still there. She did not answer them. She put the box on the table instead of under it, where she would have to see it in the morning. It was not a promise to do better. It was a decision to stop pretending the letters had vanished.

IV.

By November the wind was sharper, and a neighbor had told her how to arrange the logs so the stove drew properly even when the east wind came. The instruction involved leaving an air gap that had looked, at first, like wasted space. Mara found this satisfying. So much of the house worked by allowing room for something invisible to move.

At the end of one long afternoon the heron returned. Mara saw it through the sitting-room window as she was washing a plate. It stood in the same channel as before, though she could not know it was the same bird. She made the obvious assumption, then noticed herself making it. She dried her hands and went to the window. The bird lifted one foot, held it above the water, and put it down again without taking a step.

Mara waited for a little while. Nothing happened that she could have described as an event. Eventually she went back to the plate. She was not disappointed. The bird had not come to provide her with a story, and for once she had not tried to demand one.
