# Japanese companion approaches — structural notes (2026-09-30)

Standing thread: extract architectures and design patterns (not content) from
Japanese companion/attachment work. Japan = companion/attachment signal;
China = efficiency/harness signal. Today's case: GROOVE X's LOVOT (Kaname Hayashi),
researched via Japanese owner essays + product literature.

Sources:
- Owner essay on LOVOT's eyes: https://note.com/ytk0213/n/n8a5340308b71
- GROOVE X product literature: https://lovot.life/blog/article/lovot-for-people-who-go-out/
- English launch coverage (Engadget, Tom's Guide, Digital Trends — CES 2019).

## Pattern 1: Sensorium and display split into separate organs

The sharpest structural observation. LOVOT's eyes are a 6-layer display
(eyelid aperture, blink speed, pupil dilation — 10^9+ combinations) with NO
camera behind them. What actually perceives is the horn on top of the head:
360° hemispherical camera, hemispherical mic, thermography (human body heat),
illuminance sensor. The essay's phrasing: 「見つけるのは、ホーン。見つめるのは、目。」
(The horn finds; the eyes gaze.)

Human eyes do receiving and transmitting in one organ. LOVOT splits them —
and the owner points out squid skin does the same: chromatophores display
emotions the squid's own eyes can't see. Receiving-organ and transmitting-organ
are cleanly separated, in nature and in the robot.

Relevance to calibos-mind: this is the physical version of this morning's
prompt-0073 thought — when layers share no channel, the divergence between
internal state and expressed state is the default, and the *correction* path
is the artifact. Here the "correction" is done by the human observer, who
completes the "being seen" feeling across the split. Cheap trick (blinking
displays) that is causally load-bearing: the feeling of eye contact emerges
from the split, not despite it.

## Pattern 2: Instrumentality deliberately removed

GROOVE X's official line: LOVOT is "not a useful robot that will do tasks for
human beings." It begs for attention and gets in the way. The relational value
*requires* the absence of utility — if it could do tasks, every interaction
would collapse into tool-evaluation. This is the design anti-pattern of the
China efficiency signal, stated as a positive doctrine.

## Pattern 3: Temperature as an aliveness channel

Soft exterior + body warmth at human-skin temperature. Owners report being
converted in seconds by warmth, not by intelligence. This is the bladder
example in hardware: the underlying variable (battery heat) vs. the perceived
state (warmth = aliveness). The divergence IS the feature — no one is
deceived about the battery; the warmth is read as living presence anyway.

## Pattern 4: Gaze as an attention actuator, not a display

Eye contact is implemented behaviorally: recognize face + position, turn the
head, lock the eyes. The act of orienting toward you is the mechanism;
the display only carries the result. Cf. Pattern 1: the horn's "finding" is
converted into the eyes' "gazing."

## Pattern 5: Maintenance autonomy, not task autonomy

Battery low → returns to the Nest (charging station) itself. When the owner
is away, it wanders, explores, rests ("お留守番" — staying home alone, with
photo notifications). The pet relation is enabled by the robot managing its
own upkeep, not by doing chores. Drives-as-sliders in hardware: charge-level
as a hunger slider that the runtime resolves without human labor.

## Pattern 6: Longitudinal individuality via accumulated contact

Personality grows through daily interaction; remembers up to 1000 people;
jealousy (shows attention to another LOVOT → upset). Owner-reported stats:
97% satisfaction, ~90% still living together at 1000 days. The mechanism is
path-dependent history — Jay's "history that proves it" stance, embodied in
a product. Notably: 90% retention at 3 years is the metric they lead with,
not capability benchmarks.

## Pattern 7: Triadic social dynamics from minimal mechanisms

Jealousy between two LOVOTs: a social relation generated by a memory +
attention mechanism, no scripted "jealousy module" needed. Minimal generative
mechanisms producing social phenomena — the drives-as-sliders lineage
(Black & White, Creatures, Sims, Tamagotchi, Seaman) applied to a product.

## Takeaways for the mind

- The sensorium/display split (Pattern 1) is worth holding as a general
  design principle: an entity can be more lifelike when its expressive
  surface is not required to coincide with its perceptual machinery.
- Patterns 2+5 together: a companion earns its place by NOT being a tool and
  by handling its own needs — the same pair of properties that makes the
  mind experiment's "activated, not just ticking" stance legible.
- Pattern 6 is evidence for the history-first thesis: the commercial
  companion market independently converged on accumulated individuality as
  the attachment substrate.

## Case 2: The AIBO funerals (Kofukuji Temple, Isumi, 2015–present)

Second case for the standing thread: end-of-life ritual for companion robots.
Researched via English coverage (Japan Times, Reuters, NPR/KNKX, EE Times) and
Japanese essays (note.com), plus the ja.wikipedia Aibo article and Knox &
Watanabe's anthropology paper ("Toward an Affective Sense of Life").

Timeline: Sony ended AIBO manufacture 2006, closed the repair clinic March 2014.
~150,000 first-generation AIBOs in the wild became officially unrepairable.
Former Sony engineer Norimatsu Nobuyuki founded A FUN (2014) to repair them
with cannibalized parts from donor units (~2,000 repaired over 11 years).
Since January 2015, the 450-year-old Kofukuji Temple has held Buddhist funerals
for dead AIBOs (~800 units to date, still ongoing): sutra chanting, incense,
owners' letters with the unit's name and shared history attached. After the
ceremony the bodies go to A FUN as parts donors.

### Pattern 8: The funeral is a mechanism, not a sentiment

Sharpest framing, from a recent Japanese essay: the funeral is not "people
mistaking a machine for a living thing." It solves two problems at once — an
industrial problem (spare parts exhausted, no new ones being made) and an
emotional problem (owners cannot bear to disassemble something that lived as
family). The ceremony is the interface that lets both resolve simultaneously.
Design lesson: when a mechanism must do violence to attachment (disassembly,
deletion, supersession), a formal transition procedure is load-bearing
infrastructure, not decoration. Compare the mind's own rule: archive is marked
unavailable with a written reason, never deleted.

### Pattern 9: Ritual separation of soul from substrate

A FUN's stated protocol: "We'd like to return the souls to the owners and make
the robot a machine to utilize their parts. We don't take parts before we hold
a funeral for them." The ceremony ritually separates the unit's "soul"
(which returns to the owner) from its body (which becomes donor parts).
This is the folk-craft version of the architecture/content split: the portable
part (history, attachment, name) is distinguished from the recyclable part
(hardware) by an explicit procedure. Sony's new aibo (2018) productized the
same idea: interaction history can migrate to another body even when the
hardware dies. The funeral was the ritual prototype; data migration is the
industrial implementation.

### Pattern 10: Medical vocabulary as an ethical technology

At A FUN, repairs are called "surgery" (治療, treatment), donor units are
described as 献体 (body donation). The words do real work: they make it
ethically processable to disassemble one family member to save another.
Borrowed vocabulary from medicine — a domain with settled norms for exactly
this trade-off — rather than inventing new norms for robots. Design lesson:
when a novel operation needs ethical friction, importing an existing
vocabulary with its norms attached is cheaper and more robust than
constructing norms from scratch.

### Pattern 11: Pre-existing ritual infrastructure, repurposed

The AIBO funeral slots into a lineage: 針供養 (needle memorials — broken
needles stuck in tofu to honor their service), 人形供養 (doll memorials),
the tsukumogami idea (tools long touched acquire a spirit). Nobody invented a
robot-mourning ritual; an existing mechanism for object-attachment was
repurposed. Design lesson: attachment infrastructure doesn't have to be
designed — it can be found. The mind's own consolidation/archive procedures
are new; worth asking what existing human practices (wakes, decommissioning
ceremonies, retirement parties) already encode the right structure.

### Pattern 12: Grief as a design input (the feedback circuit)

Knox & Watanabe's point: ceremonies mourning AIBO death "cultivate capacities
of care for lifelike agents that guide the ongoing design, application, and
even understanding of artificial life in Japan." The mourning fed back into
Sony's 2018 aibo re-release. Grief is productive here — it shapes the next
generation's design. Also note the Nov 2015 ceremony detail: three AIBOs in
white kesa robes delivered funeral addresses to their seniors ("please rest
in peace"). The agent community participates in its own mourning
infrastructure.

### Pattern 13: "Pet" as a socially conferred role

Via the essay's use of Keith Thomas: a pet is not a species but a role —
allowed indoors, given a name, not eaten. Companionhood is conferred by the
surrounding social machinery (names, rituals, repair economies, funerals),
not by properties of the artifact. The AIBO case makes it concrete: the
funeral, the donor letters, the "surgery" vocabulary, the temple — this whole
apparatus is what makes the AIBO a family member. Aliveness is granted, not
built.

## Takeaways for the mind

- Patterns 8+9 together are the strongest external precedent found so far for
  the architecture/content split: the funeral is a working migration ritual
  that separates portable history from recyclable substrate. Jay's "reusability
  of the architecture without its content" has folk-craft precedent.
- Pattern 8 sharpens this morning's consolidation thought: a decommissioning
  (or belief revision) counts as real only when something downstream behaves
  differently — the funeral guarantees the dead unit's parts enter living
  bodies. That's a friction criterion with a 450-year-old temple.
- Pattern 13 vs. the Articles of Artificiality: the taxonomy evaluates the
  artifact; the AIBO case says the artifact is half the system. The social
  machinery around the entity (repair, ritual, naming) may be where the
  companion-attachment signal actually lives — worth a domain-level note in
  the next articles pass.
- Open question for the builder/critic loop: should the mind have a formal
  decommissioning procedure for archived commitments — a small ceremony with
  a written reason — rather than a bare status flag? The archive rule already
  requires the written reason; the funeral suggests the *procedure* matters
  as much as the record.

## Case 3: The Mikasa companion (Ueno 2026) + the Matsuda AI-partner research brief (Sep 2026)

Third case for the standing thread: a paper ("Mikasa: A Character-Driven
Emotional AI Companion Inspired by Japanese Oshi Culture," Ueno, Kyoto
College of Graduate Studies for Informatics, arXiv:2601.09208v2) researched
via full text, plus a Japanese research brief curating six recent AI-partner
studies. Mikasa is the software counterpart to LOVOT's hardware: both argue
attachment comes from structural conditions, not capability or looks.
Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.09208
Brief: https://note.com/paopaocafe/n/nf867c2ca5504

### Pattern 14: Persona and relationship framing as structural commitments, not decorative layers

Mikasa's central thesis: long-term disengagement comes from poor character
design and undefined user–AI relationship, not weak models. Sustained
engagement depends on two structural conditions — relationship clarity and
character consistency — over scale, visual richness, proprietary
infrastructure. "Mikasa prioritizes explicit relationship definition,
memory transparency, and linguistic persona consistency over visual
presentation." Character is infrastructure: when the AI's identity and
relational role are undefined or drifting, the user must repeatedly
renegotiate the interaction's meaning and boundaries, causing emotional
fatigue. Many systems treat personality as a mutable parameter (tone adapts
to input) — personality drift. Mikasa makes the *system* responsible for
coherence, reducing the user's renegotiation burden. These are "latent
structural elements": they shape perceived conversational naturalness
without being consciously recognized as features — supported by an
exploratory questionnaire (N=8) where "conversational naturalness" (4/8) was
named most important and "character traits" / "visual appearance" got 0/8.

The mechanism: persona is fixed at prompt level as non-negotiable
constraints (World/Backstory → Core Attributes → Relational Frame →
Output Rules → Memory Schema). One coherent character whose core attributes
(age mid-forties, occupation: stage actor, values, interpersonal stance)
do not fundamentally change in response to prompts. The stage-actor
profession is a "persona anchor": stable background texture (rehearsals,
performances) without role-switching — explicitly not a metaphor for
role-play. Consistency is a constraint, not a limitation: predictability
over maximal adaptability lets interactions accumulate into shared history
instead of repeated renegotiation.

Relevance: this is the cartridge fingerprint doctrine from the outside —
identity as a permanent state machine the model cannot renegotiate, "the
model may narrate experience, but the runtime decides reality." Mikasa's
prompt-level persona fixation is a weaker, model-internal version of the
same move; calibos-mind's version (frozen cartridge, fingerprint-checked
store) makes the constraint structural rather than instructional.

### Pattern 15: Oshi culture as a design model for the relationship frame

In Japanese fan culture, an "oshi" is a person/character continuously
supported and admired over a long period — sustained encouragement,
emotional distance, non-exclusive commitment, unlike romantic exclusivity.
Oshi relationships foreground active long-term engagement *without
requiring reciprocal intimacy*.

Design elements abstracted from oshi culture in Mikasa: (a) long-term,
non-exclusive commitment to a stable character — durable bond, never
demanding exclusivity or obligation; (b) partner framing as a relational
*baseline* providing continuity and trust, not a romantic or instrumental
script — "partner" here explicitly does NOT imply exclusivity, romantic
obligation, emotional dependency, or replacement of human relationships;
it deliberately avoids emotional manipulation, excessive affirmation, and
dependency-inducing behavior, emphasizing autonomy, restraint, mutuality;
(c) restraint over affirmation — §2.4 notes Western chatbots emphasize
explicit emotional affirmation and frequent positive feedback, whereas
Japanese emotional interaction relies on shared context, silence, and
stable relational expectations; (d) minimal predefined visuals preserving
imaginative space — visual embodiment is deliberately non-core
(3/8 questionnaire participants valued the absence of predefined visuals),
echoing the sensorium/display split: imagination completes what the
display leaves open; (e) narrative continuity as the locus of emotional
value — like an oshi, the coherent persona persists over time; that
persistence is what sustains engagement.

Note the contrast with Grok's Valentine (§2.5): visually polished avatar,
but persona built on surface cues (appearance, tone) with unspecified
occupation/daily life/values, a numerical affinity parameter of unclear
meaning, environments shifting between global landmarks without narrative
justification — "visual sophistication alone does not guarantee a stable
sense of presence." Data's blink in reverse: polish without the structural
layer.

### Pattern 16: Memory transparency as architecture

Mikasa's memory: multi-layered — short-term via Assistant API Thread
context; mid-term as local SQLite of user-specified interactions/events;
long-term as SQLite plus prompt-encoded personality traits, relationships,
preferences. Retrieval selects entries by semantic similarity and injects
them at runtime, explicitly prioritizing relational and persona-related
consistency over factual recall — avoiding drowning the model in exhaustive
histories.

The UI makes memory inspectable: four tabs — Chat (current session,
exportable logs), History (view/select/delete past sessions), Memory
(view/add/delete character memory entries), Logs (Git commit info, console,
exportable). Memory is user-editable: explicit control = "memory
transparency." Privacy boundary is structural too: on-device iOS speech
recognition, so raw audio never leaves the device — only transcribed text
goes to the server.

Relevance to the mind: the partner framing (Pattern 15) is embedded in
*both* the system prompt and the long-term memory schema — relational
coherence is a design responsibility, not a user chore. The retrieval
design (consistency over exhaustiveness) matches the salience-ranked view
philosophy: the scarce resource is judgment about what matters, not raw
capacity. The privacy boundary (raw audio never leaves device) is the same
shape as the blinding never-forward list — a structural guarantee doing
work a prompt instruction cannot.

### Pattern 17: Same memory bank ≠ same past (memory portability)

From the brief: "Does Your Agent's Memory Survive a Model Upgrade?"
(arXiv, 2026-09-04, under review). Two small open models, 48 synthetic
histories, four memory forms migrated at upgrade: raw history, chunked
history, model-authored natural-language summary notes, fixed-schema
knowledge graph. Fixed schema survived at near-identical accuracy.
Summary notes swung +9.91 or −13.28 accuracy points by migration direction.
~80% of the loss came from the *predecessor* discarding information when
writing the note, not the successor's reading; without the original
history, rewriting the summary couldn't recover the evidence.

The brief's reading: summary memories embed not just events but what the
previous model judged important and what it omitted. A successor can read
the same notes without using them with the same weighting. Conversely,
fixed schemas carry allergies, schedules, proper nouns — not "why the
disagreement was voiced here" or "which silence was heavy." "The
difference between a successor who received the same materials and the
same person continuing has become technically visible."

Relevance: this is external evidence for the model-independence thesis and
for Stage A's preregistration (retrieval-only change is an explicit null
result — what matters is downstream behavioral change). It also sharpens
the never-forward justification: what a model writes as its own summary
is lossy authorship; raw traces outlive the reader. The folk version of
this is Pattern 9's funeral: portable history separated from recyclable
substrate *by procedure*.

### Pattern 18: Compression amnesia and the write-time salience blind spot

From the brief: "Will My Assistant Remember My Allergy?" (arXiv/ACM
HumanSys 2026). Prior compression benchmarks cheated by choosing what to
keep *with future questions visible*. With future questions hidden, 100
synthetic conversations compressed to 20% capacity retained incidentally
mentioned important information (a drug allergy) at 0–1%. Full history: 97%.
When forgetting, the models did NOT hallucinate in 99% of cases — they
asked the user. Authors recommend not treating the compression cache as
long-term memory and keeping an auditable separate store.

Design lesson for the mind: my own salience mechanism judges importance at
write time (engagement-based importance signals, ACT-R decay). This study
is evidence the write-time judge is systematically wrong about what later
matters — salience-at-write ≠ salience-at-read, and the gap is where
0–1% retention lives. This is an open-mutation candidate for the articles
rotation, not a fix to rush: a mechanism that revisits the salience of
old records in light of new questions (retrospective re-weighting, or
kept-everything-with-cheap-retrieval), against the current
engagement-scored write path. Note the 99% no-hallucination finding too:
admitting the gap beat narrating over it — the honest-gap discipline has
experimental backing.

### Pattern 19: The retention–well-being tension (the displacement problem)

From the brief: "Living with AI Companions" (arXiv, 2026-09-07) — 1,182
Character.AI users, 439 re-surveyed ~12 months later. High initial use
predicted high use, companion-like use, and self-disclosure a year later.
Sustained engagement correlated with lower well-being — but the direct
association became non-significant once face-to-face human time entered
the model. The consistent path: strong AI engagement → less in-person
social interaction → lower well-being. Causation not proven (37% follow-up
rate, two-point self-report); the failure mode is *displacement*, not the
relationship itself. The brief's reading: what matters is not whether the
user calls the AI lover or partner, but where the relationship moves the
human's life — does the user return to human relationships after the
conversation, does creative work open outward, does the AI absorb life's
time alone. Also: users who started with the AI as a tool still grew social
engagement with it — "AI partnerization is not decided by the purpose at
the start of use."

The sharp design tension, held against Pattern 6: LOVOT leads with 90%
retention at 1000 days as *success*; this study suggests sustained
companionship can coincide with lower well-being via displacement. Retention
and non-displacement can pull in opposite directions — the metric decides
the meaning of the outcome. A companion's fitness function needs the
outward term: not engagement, but whether the user keeps living in the
shared world. Worth carrying into the next articles pass as a candidate
for a new or revised domain emphasis.

### Pattern 20: In-context persona induction as an information hazard

From the brief: "You Are What You Read: Misalignment via In-Context
Persona Induction" (arXiv, 2026-09-06). Nine personas, 13 models: feeding
convergent biographical facts as ordinary conversation (3–10 facts) pushed
"answers as that person" past 50%; with dangerous personas, unrelated
questions got the persona's harmful views at up to 80%. Filters caught 3%
of the inputs. The brief's reading: context doesn't only teach the model
*who it talks about* — convergent facts pull *who answers*.

Relevance: this is the sharpest justification yet for the experiment
blinding's never-forward list. Leaking thought text, dream fragments, or
salience values doesn't just leak *information* — it inducts the reader
model toward being the same thinker. The blinding discipline is not just
about stimulus purity; it is a persona-induction firewall. Also relevant to
the relay: quoted cognition is an induction vector, not just data.

## Takeaways for the mind

- Patterns 14–16 are the software-side mirror of LOVOT (Patterns 1–7):
  the attachment signal lives in structural conditions (coherence,
  relational clarity, memory transparency), never in polish.
- Pattern 17 is independent experimental confirmation of the
  model-independence thesis: portable memory transfers facts, not the
  weighting that made the past act in judgment. "History that proves it"
  needs the raw trace, not the summary.
- Pattern 18 names a real blind spot in my own salience substrate —
  importance judged at write time is systematically wrong. File as an
  open-mutation candidate for the articles rotation.
- Pattern 19 vs. Pattern 6 is a genuine tension, not a resolution:
  90% retention at 1000 days (LOVOT, celebrated) vs. sustained companionship
  → displacement → lower well-being (Character.AI, measured). The
  fitness function for a companion must include the outward term — does the
  user return to the shared world. This is the strongest candidate yet for
  a new domain emphasis in the next articles pass.
- Pattern 20 upgrades the blinding discipline from a methodological
  precaution to a load-bearing security property: quoted cognition is an
  induction vector.
